When Fabric DirectQuery Felt Faster Than Snowflake — A Real Power BI Experience

A real Power BI DirectQuery experience where Fabric felt faster than Snowflake — and why BI performance isn’t just about the database.

The observation that made me pause

If you read most discussions online, you’ll often see Snowflake described as a strong and mature analytics platform. And that reputation exists for good reasons.

That’s why I paused when, in one of my real Power BI projects, a DirectQuery report backed by Fabric Lakehouse / Warehouse felt more responsive than the same report backed by Snowflake — even with a dataset larger than 120 GB.

This wasn’t a lab test or a benchmark.
It was a real dashboard, real users, and real interactions.

And the difference was noticeable enough to make me curious.

The setup (kept simple and realistic)

To explain this clearly, here’s the high-level setup:

  • Option 1:
    Power BI using DirectQuery on Snowflake
  • Option 2:
    Power BI using DirectQuery on Fabric Lakehouse / Warehouse
    (data ingested through Dataflows Gen2)

The reports were similar.
The business logic was similar.
The users were the same.

Yet day-to-day interactions — slicers, filters, and visual refreshes — felt smoother with Fabric.

My first reaction: this shouldn’t be the case

From a pure data platform point of view, Snowflake is extremely capable. It handles large analytical workloads very well and is used at massive scale.

So my first thought wasn’t, “Fabric is better.”
My first thought was, “What am I really observing here?”

That question changed how I looked at the problem.

What “felt faster” actually means in BI

In BI, performance is rarely about a single SQL query running fast.

Most users don’t care about query execution time in isolation.
They care about:

  • how quickly a slicer responds
  • how fast visuals refresh
  • whether filters feel instant or laggy
  • how consistent the experience feels during the day

In other words, perceived performance matters more than raw engine speed.

That’s an important distinction.

Why this can happen (without saying one platform is better)

After digging deeper, it became clear that this wasn’t about one system being “stronger” than another.

It was about how close each system sits to Power BI and how the full path of a request behaves.

A few factors stood out:

  • Power BI and Fabric live within the same ecosystem, which can reduce network hops.
  • DirectQuery workloads often generate many small, interactive queries rather than large batch queries.
  • Fabric Warehouse is designed with Power BI interaction patterns in mind.
  • Snowflake, while very powerful, sits outside the Power BI service boundary.

None of this makes one platform superior.
It simply explains why the experience can feel different.

What this does not mean

It’s important to be clear about what I am not claiming.

This experience does not mean:

  • Fabric is faster than Snowflake in general
  • Snowflake performs poorly with Power BI
  • one platform should replace the other everywhere

This was not a benchmark.
It was one real setup, observed over real usage.

How I think about platform choice after this experience

This experience didn’t change my respect for Snowflake as a data platform.

What it did reinforce is this:

  • Snowflake works extremely well as a general-purpose analytics engine used by many tools.
  • Fabric can feel very efficient when Power BI is the primary consumer and DirectQuery interactivity matters.

The right choice depends on how the data is used, not just where it is stored.

The bigger lesson I took from this

This reminded me of something I’ve seen many times in data work:

The tool that looks best on paper doesn’t always create the best user experience.

Performance is an end-to-end story:

  • data location
  • integration
  • query patterns
  • user behavior

Focusing on only one layer often misses the real reason something feels slow or fast.

Why curiosity matters here

If I had relied only on common assumptions, I might have ignored this difference.

Instead, curiosity made me ask:

  • Why does this feel faster?
  • Where is time actually being spent?
  • What part of the system matters most to the user?

That mindset has helped me more than any single tool or feature.
