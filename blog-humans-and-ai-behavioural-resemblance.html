Resemblance Between Humans and AI in behavioural: is it growing or mirrored?

A missing piece of business context changed how I think about defaults, inaction, and our belief on time and choice.

PhilosophySelf AwarenessArtificial IntelligenceDataPersonal Growth

A missing piece of business context changed how I think about defaults, inaction, and our belief on time and choice.

I was trying to understand why there was a sudden spike of one course for a category it doesn’t belong to. By business rules, this course belongs to category “Courseware" and the data shows me an upward spike of it under the “Microlearning" category. I had already check the data model and ETL pipeline, but the numbers still did not make sense. So I gave my AI assistant the relevant structure, explained the mismatch, and asked it to help me find what I had missed.

Its response arrived quickly. The reasoning was orderly, the steps were practical, and the proposed solution looked convincing. I tried one suggestion, added another detail, and asked a follow-up question. Each answer became more specific.

And suddenly I realised that it was solving the wrong problem.

The missing detail was the business rule of how categories were defined or formulated. It was not obscure information. It was so familiar to me that I had stopped noticing it as context. I knew it, the people around the process knew it, and somewhere along the way I had mistaken familiar knowledge for shared knowledge.

The AI had answered the problem I described, not the problem I meant.

What stayed with me was not that an AI system had made an incorrect assumption but how easily the conversation continued after that assumption.

I kept supplying more information, and the system kept producing more polished answers. Yet every new answer inherited the same missing premise. More conversation made the proposed solution more elaborate; it did not make it more accurate.

The system could have asked a clarifying question. It did not. I could have stopped earlier and examined what I had left unsaid. I did not.

When I finally supplied the missing business rule, the direction changed. The correction did not emerge because enough minutes had passed or because enough responses had accumulated. It appeared only after the context changed.

That experience gave a practical meaning to a familiar sentence: not choosing is also choosing.

The statement needs precision. An unspecified detail does not always matter. But when a process must continue and a material decision is left blank, the blank rarely remains neutral. A person, a system, a convention, or a probability fills it. By not choosing the missing context, we quietly delegate the choice to a default or to another identity.

Data work contains another version of the same pattern. A deadline approaches, so someone adds a temporary exception to a report or a manual step to a process. There is usually a reasonable explanation: we will correct it after the release, after the quarter closes, or when the source system changes.

Then other work begins to depend on it.

The temporary rule is copied into another report. Someone builds a dashboard that assumes it will always be there. A new colleague inherits the process without knowing why the exception exists. Nobody holds a meeting and decides that the workaround should become permanent.

Permanence is created through a series of postponed decisions.

Time does not repair the workaround. It gives the workaround opportunities to acquire dependencies.

That distinction followed me beyond work.

Time gives patterns somewhere to live

Pain can create temporary rules too.

After being hurt, silence may feel safer than explanation. Distance may feel safer than trust. Handling everything alone may feel safer than needing another person. These responses are not foolish or weak. In the circumstances that created them, they may have been intelligent forms of protection.

The difficulty begins when a temporary protection continues after the conditions have changed.

New people arrive, but an old fear interprets them.
New opportunities appear, but an old conclusion decides what they mean.
More years bring more experiences, yet those experiences can still be organised around the same unexamined premise.

Like an AI conversation built on incomplete context, a life can become increasingly coherent around something that is no longer completely true.

This is why saying “time heals” has never felt sufficient to me. Time can soften intensity. It can create distance from an event, bring supportive people into our lives, and offer experiences that were previously unavailable. These are real contributions. Time is not irrelevant.

But time is the facilitator, not the healer. Not the doer.

Time cannot name the need we have never acknowledged. It cannot set a boundary, reconsider a belief, begin an honest conversation, ask for help, or allow grief that has repeatedly been postponed. It creates room in which those things may become possible. It does not perform them for us.

Conscious action turns that room into participation.

That action does not always look dramatic. It may be speaking, leaving, apologising, seeking professional help, changing a repeated response, or allowing ourselves to be supported. Sometimes it is deliberate rest. Sometimes it is admitting that we do not yet feel safe enough to make a larger change.

A conscious pause is not the same as unconscious avoidance.

This matters because “not choosing is also choosing” can become cruel when used carelessly. Fear, trauma, exhaustion, dependence, and lack of safety can severely limit a person’s choices. A frozen nervous system is not a moral failure. Responsibility should not be confused with blame.

Once you become aware of something, you have the ability to choose differently and you still you don’t question your old habits, those habits will keep controlling your actions.

We may not be responsible for the wound that created a pattern. But, when we have enough safety and support, we can participate in deciding whether that pattern remains our architecture.

When I see such architecture designed by indecisive choices, I wonder if we are doing the same what we call as “AI hallucination”?

OR AI has learned that from our behaviour?

But before this confuses as “control each action”, know - Action is not the same as control

This understanding brought me back to a verse from the Bhagavad Gita:

कर्मण्येवाधिकारस्ते मा फलेषु कदाचन।
मा कर्मफलहेतुर्भूर्मा ते सङ्गोऽस्त्वकर्मणि॥ २.४७॥

Your domain is action, not a complete ownership of its results.
Do not make the result your only motive, and do not become attached to inaction.

The final phrase is especially direct:

मा ते सङ्गोऽस्त्वकर्मणि

Do not become attached to inaction.

The verse does not promise that every sincere action will produce the result we want. It does not tell us that failure is evidence of insufficient effort. Its boundary is subtler: action belongs within our sphere of participation; outcomes do not belong entirely within our control; and uncertainty is not a reason to make inaction our refuge.

That is also where fate fits for me.

Fate should not become the first explanation we reach for before we have participated fully. There are outcomes that effort cannot guarantee and circumstances that individual action cannot overcome. But only after sincere participation meets those limits, I can honestly recognise what was beyond my control.

Conscious action is not control over life. It is a refusal to confuse waiting with healing.

Back at my screen, the proposed solution changed when I supplied the missing context. Human life is far more complex than an AI conversation, and healing is not prompt engineering. Still, the observation remains with me.

The passage of time gives us another day, another conversation, another opportunity to notice what has been operating quietly. Awareness helps us see the default. Action gives us a chance to revise our relationship with it.

Time provides the space. It does not make the choice.

The question I am left with is not whether enough time has passed.

It is this: what in my life began as temporary procrastination but has quietly become architecture?
